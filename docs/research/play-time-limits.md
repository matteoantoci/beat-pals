# Play-time limits and healthy sessions for a child of 4 to 5

The parents fear that Beat Pals can become compulsive for the child. This file collects the guidance on play time for ages 2 to 5. It also collects the research on how young children stop screen play, the design codes, and what other products do. It ends with design options for Beat Pals.

Research date: 2026-11-26. "[UNVERIFIED]" marks a claim that I could not trace to a primary source. "[INFERENCE]" marks my own conclusion.

## Summary: the gold standard

1. **At most about 1 hour of screen time each day for ages 2 to 5, and less is better.** WHO, the Canadian Paediatric Society, Sweden, and the UK (2026) give this number. France goes further: for ages 3 to 6, screens are "not recommended and exceptional", and only with an adult. The AAP moved in 2026 from minute counts to the quality and the design of the media. (Section 1)
2. **Screens together, never as a comfort, and not before sleep.** Every guideline asks for co-use with an adult, no screens in the hour before bed, and no screens in the bedroom or at meals. Some guidelines also say: do not use a screen to calm the child. (Section 1)
3. **The product ends the session at a natural stopping point, as a routine.** Children are less upset when the technology ends the session than when a parent ends it. A spoken "two more minutes" from a parent goes with more upset, not less. When a child plans the session and the product keeps the plan, the child stops alone 93% of the time. (Section 2)
4. **No design that works against the limit.** Autoplay, endless play, streaks, intermittent rewards, lures, and characters that pressure the child to stay are the named problems. The ICO code, the EU guidelines for the DSA, and the AAP name them. (Sections 3 and 5)
5. **The parent sets the limit, and the product makes the stop easy.** The strongest products put the limit behind a parent gate and stop with a clear, friendly end. The best tested design for this age ends after the last planned item, with a character that says goodbye. (Sections 2 and 4)

**Quality against quantity.** The guidance agrees on a maximum of about 1 hour. But the newer texts put more weight on the content and the design. The UK asks for slow-paced, predictable content. Sweden and the AAP 2026 policy warn against design that pulls the child to use the media more and more.

**What counts.** The WHO counts "sedentary screen time" as "time spent passively watching screen-based entertainment". It does not count "active screen-based games where physical activity or movement is required" ([WHO executive summary](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/60a1cbaa-2bef-4251-9557-e52ce22112b3/content)). A tap game in a chair is close to sedentary screen time. So Beat Pals time counts against the 1 hour, together with television and video. [INFERENCE]

## 2. How preschool children stop screen play

### 2.1 Hiniker et al. 2016, "Screen Time Tantrums" (CHI)

Interviews with 27 parents and a diary study with 28 families (380 screen sessions) ([DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858278](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858278), [PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/ScreenTimeTantrums.pdf)).

- 93% of the parents said that the child throws a tantrum, whines, or resists the end of screen time at least occasionally. 37% said that screen time almost always ends with a fight.
- **Technology against parent.** Children were less upset when "the technology turned itself off" (mean 2.98) than when the parent turned it off (mean 3.47, p = .006). The authors conclude that "technology may be an effective third-party mediator for easing transition pain".
- **Warnings.** Children "were significantly more upset about transitions when they were warned by parents that screen time would be ending" (3.35 against 3.03, p < .001). The effect stayed after the authors removed one confound. Note: these warnings came from parents, in an observational study. The study did not test a warning from the product. [INFERENCE]
- **Routine.** Transitions were easier when screen time was "a routine part of the day" (2.84 against 3.10, p < .001). Routine sessions were longer: 40.0 min against 29.5 min.
- **Natural stopping points.** "Parents find episode, playlist, and movie end points to be easy boundaries to enforce, and children experience these as natural stopping points." Autoplay works against them: "If the new one starts, then he's usually very upset."

### 2.2 Hiniker et al. 2017, "Plan & Play" (IDC)

The child plans the apps of a session before play. A system dialog tells the child when the plan says to move on ([DOI 10.1145/3078072.3079752](https://doi.org/10.1145/3078072.3079752), [PDF](http://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/PlanAndPlay.pdf)).

- Lab study with 11 parent-child pairs, interviews with 14 parents.
- "When children played through their plan, they transitioned to the next activity without intervention 93% of the time."
- Parents valued "its ability to give children agency and ownership within defined parameters".

### 2.3 Hiniker et al. 2018, "Coco's Videos" (CHI)

A video app for preschool children. Three versions ran for 3 weeks in 24 homes: neutral, a lock-out after the plan, and "post-play" (autoplay of more videos after the plan) ([DOI 10.1145/3173574.3173828](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173828), [PDF](http://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/CocosVideos.pdf)).

- "Post-play significantly reduced children's autonomy and likelihood of self-regulation, extended video-viewing time, and led to increases in parent intervention."
- "The lock-out mechanism did not reduce video-viewing time or the likelihood of parent intervention." So the hard lock gave little. The plan and the end ritual did the work. [INFERENCE]
- The end ritual: at the start of the last video, the video pauses and an alarm clock shows. Audio: "We're almost done! Get ready to say goodbye when this video ends." One minute before the end: "One minute left, then it's time to say goodbye."
- Children came to expect the ritual. One child asked: "Is she gonna say, 'Time to say goodbye'?" Coco asked "Are you ready for bed?", and the child said "Yeah" and began to prepare for bed.

### 2.4 Other studies and guidance on the stop

- **Manipulative design in apps for ages 3 to 5** (Radesky et al. 2022, JAMA Netw Open, 160 children, 133 apps; [DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186)). Only 20% of the apps had no manipulative design. The designs to prolong play:
  - characters that pressure the child (24.8% of apps)
  - time pressure (17.3%)
  - navigation constraints, for example auto-advance with no stop option (45.9%)
  - lures, for example tokens, rewards, and virtual toys (45.1%).
- **Devices to calm a child** (Radesky et al. 2023, JAMA Pediatrics, 422 children aged 3 to 5; [DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4793](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4793)). Among boys, frequent use of devices to calm went with higher emotional reactivity later.
- **Tablet use and anger** (Fitzpatrick et al. 2024, JAMA Pediatrics; [DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2024.2511](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2024.2511)). The AAP 2026 policy cites this line of research as "Heavier tablet use is linked with more anger outbursts over time." I read only the AAP summary, not the paper. [UNVERIFIED for the effect size]
- **AAP tip sheet on transitions** ([PDF](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/COE_Help-your-young-child-transition-away-from-screens.pdf)): "It's usually easier to break after an episode has finished, not in the middle of one." It also advises an offline activity to go to next, a predictable time of day, and "a code word or phrase to help your child know it's time to stop".

## 3. Design codes and regulation

None of these texts applies to Beat Pals by law. Beat Pals is a private family app with no personal data and no platform. They are still the best public statement of good design for children. [INFERENCE]

### 3.1 UK ICO, Age Appropriate Design Code

- **Standard 5, detrimental use of data.** "Do not use children's personal data in ways that have been shown to be detrimental to their wellbeing." The guidance names "'sticky' features" such as "reward loops, continuous scrolling, notifications and auto-play features". It suggests "pause buttons which allow children to take a break at any time without losing their progress in a game" ([ICO, standard 5](https://cy.ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)).
- **Standard 13, nudge techniques.** The core rule is about privacy: no nudges that "lead or encourage children to activate options that mean they give you more of their personal data". For ages 0 to 5, the table adds: "Nudge towards wellbeing enhancing behaviours (such as taking breaks). Provide tools to support wellbeing enhancing behaviours (such as mid-level pause and save features)" ([ICO, standard 13](https://cy.ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/)).
- **ICO tips for game designers.** "Introduce checkpoints, automatic periodic saving of progress, or natural breaks in play between game matches." Help players "disengage from extended sessions without feeling pressurised to continue playing" ([ICO](https://cy.ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/top-tips-for-games-designers-how-to-comply-with-the-children-s-code/)).

### 3.2 EU, guidelines on the protection of minors under DSA Article 28 (2025)

Published on 14 July 2025, in the Official Journal as C/2025/5519 ([Commission page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors), [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML?uri=CELEX:52025XC05519)).

- **Default settings (section 6.3.1).** "The default autoplay of videos and hosting live streams are turned off." Push notifications are off by default "and are always off during core sleep hours". Features "that may contribute to excessive use", such as "streaks", are turned off.
- **Interface design (section 6.4).** Minors must not meet "persuasive design features that are aimed predominantly at engagement". Examples: endless scroll, "automatic triggering of video content", notifications "artificially timed to regain minors' attention", and "the creation of virtual rewards for performing (repeated) actions on the platform".
- **Time management tools (section 6.4).** The guidelines ask for "customisable, visible, easy-to access and use, child-friendly and effective time management tools". "These tools should deter minors from spending more time on the platform."
- **Commercial practices (section 6.6).** No "intermittent or random rewards" that "can lead to excessive, impulsive or unwanted spending or addictive behaviours".

### 3.3 5Rights, AAP 2026, and Designed with Kids in Mind

- **5Rights, the right to informed and conscious use:** "Children and young people should be empowered to reach into creative places online, but at the same time have the capacity and support to easily disengage" ([5Rights framework](https://5rightsframework.com/4-2)). Its overview lists "endless scrolls", "gambling-like features", and "constant nudges and reminders" as features that keep children engaged ([5Rights](https://5rightsfoundation.com/the-problem)).
- **AAP 2026:** engagement business models use "algorithmic recommender systems, autoplay, intermittent rewards" ([policy, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13122503)). The AAP asks industry for "turning off autoplay" by default and for "minimizing designs that prolong engagement" ([AAP release](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2026/american-academy-of-pediatrics-calls-for-creating-a-child-friendly-digital-world)).
- **Designed with Kids in Mind** (a US campaign for a design code): autoplay "promotes family conflict/battles over turning off". The fix: "Autoplay on children's videos or content should be off by default, making it easier for children to put devices down and adults to enforce limits" ([campaign page](https://designedwithkidsinmind.us/)).

### 3.4 Apple App Store, Kids Category

Rule 1.3: Kids Category apps "must not include links out of the app, purchasing opportunities, or other distractions to kids unless reserved for a designated area behind a parental gate" ([App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Rule 1.3 says nothing about play time. Beat Pals already puts the parent area behind a parent gate (P14).

## 5. The compulsion side, and Beat Pals

| Mechanic that drives compulsive use | Named by | In Beat Pals? |
|---|---|---|
| Autoplay or post-play: the next item starts by itself | Hiniker 2016 and 2018, AAP 2026, DSA guidelines, Designed with Kids in Mind | **No.** The end scene lasts at most 5 s and then shows one button back to the song list (spec, story 38). She must choose the next song. |
| Endless play with no stopping cue | DSA guidelines (endless scroll), 5Rights, ICO standard 5 | **Partly.** Each song ends, so each song is a natural stopping point. But the session has no end. All songs are open (P12), and the song list is one tap away after each song. The title music loops on the song list (P14). |
| Variable or intermittent rewards | AAP 2026, DSA guidelines 6.6, ICO ("reward loops") | **No.** A star needs a score of 80%, a fixed rule (P12, spec). Energy steps come at fixed hit counts (P13). Star moments come from the phrases of the chart. Only their sound changes at random, within ±1 semitone (spec, sound effects). |
| Streaks, daily rewards, loss aversion | DSA guidelines 6.3.1 ("streaks"), Duolingo streak | **No streaks.** Stars are never lost (P5, P12). The band energy can drop one step inside a song (P13). That is a small loss that ends with the song. |
| Lures: tokens, prizes, a collection | Radesky 2022 (45.1% of apps) | **Small.** Decision 10 rejects a sticker book because a collection moves the motivation to the prize (Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett, 1973). But each song card shows a record color and 3 stars, so the song list is a visible collection to fill. The next star is always one play away. [INFERENCE] |
| Characters that pressure the child to stay | Radesky 2022 (24.8% of apps) | **No.** The spec has no pal that asks her to stay or looks sad when she leaves. Keep this as a rule. |
| Time pressure, countdowns | Radesky 2022 (17.3% of apps) | **No.** |
| Navigation constraints: no way out | Radesky 2022 (45.9% of apps) | **Small.** Pause works at any time. Home on the pause screen needs a hold of 0.6 s, so that a stray tap does not end the song. That protects against accidents and does not hide the exit. |
| Notifications, ads, purchases | DSA guidelines, Apple 1.3 | **No.** |
| Fast, intense stimulation | UK 2026 ("slow-paced, predictable content") | **Medium.** The music sets the pace, but the hit effects, the energy steps, and the star moments add strong effects (P13, spec). [INFERENCE] |

**Conclusion.** Beat Pals avoids the named compulsion mechanics. The gap: the session has no end, and nothing in the game says "this is enough for today". So the parent must end each session. Hiniker 2016 shows that a stop from a parent upsets a child more than a stop from the technology. [INFERENCE]

## 6. Design options for Beat Pals

The songs last about 3 minutes, so 3 songs take about 10 minutes with the song choice. [INFERENCE]

### Option 1 (recommended): a concert of N songs with a goodbye ritual

- **What the child sees.** The song list shows N lights over the stage, for example 3. Each song that she plays turns one light on. When she starts the last song, the pals hold up one finger, and a soft chime plays: "last song". After its end scene, the pals wave, bow, and lie down to sleep. The lights dim, and a lullaby plays for a few seconds. Then the title screen shows the pals asleep, with no play button.
- **The next concert.** The parent opens it with the logo hold and the question (P14). A parent setting can also open it again after a rest time, for example 2 hours.
- **Fit with the decisions.** The concert ends after a song, so the stop always comes at a natural stopping point. No song stops in the middle, nothing fails, and she loses no star (P5, P12). It adds no reward and no lure (decision 10). The parent area holds the setting (P14). It follows Coco's Videos: a cue at the start of the last item, a goodbye from a character, and a routine that the child learns.
- **Open details.** A song must count when its end scene shows. A song that she leaves from the pause screen counts only after a minimum time, for example 60 s. Otherwise a wrong choice costs her a song.
- **Effort.** About 1 day [INFERENCE]. The parts:
  - a setting in the parent area
  - a session count in the model, with tests
  - the last-song cue
  - the goodbye scene and its sounds
  - the sleep screen.
- **Later variant (Plan & Play).** Before the concert, she puts N song covers into the N slots, as a plan. Then she plays them in her order. This adds the plan step that gave 93% stops with no parent. Effort: about 1 more day. [INFERENCE]

### Option 2: a parent-set minute budget that ends after the current song

- **What the child sees.** The same goodbye as option 1. The budget runs out during a song, but the song plays to its end, and then the goodbye comes.
- **Fit.** It matches the 1-hour daily guidance in minutes, and it never cuts a song. But the child cannot see minutes. So she cannot predict the end, and the end feels random to her. [INFERENCE] The concert of option 1 shows the plan as lights.
- **Effort.** About half a day on top of option 1, because the timer must stop during pause and in the background. It works best as a cap inside option 1: "N songs or M minutes, at the end of a song". [INFERENCE]

### Option 3: a bedtime lockout

- **What the child sees.** The parent sets a clock time. After that time, the title screen shows the pals asleep and does not open the song list. A song in progress plays to its end.
- **Fit.** It applies the advice "no screens in the hour before bed" (CPS, UK, Sweden, France). It reuses the sleep screen of option 1.
- **Effort.** About 2 to 3 hours on top of option 1. Alone, it needs its own sleep screen, so about half a day. [INFERENCE]

### Option 4: OS-level limits only (Guided Access, Screen Time)

- **What the child sees with Guided Access.** If the parent turns them on, a sound and an adult voice that tells the minutes that remain. She cannot use a count of minutes. At the end, a grey system screen with text covers the pals in the middle of a song. I could not confirm if the song audio stops at that moment. [UNVERIFIED] Only the parent passcode continues.
- **What the child sees with Screen Time.** A text reminder 5 minutes before Downtime, which she cannot read. Then a dimmed app with an hourglass, or a time-limit screen with a "One More Minute" button. A child of 4 can tap that button, so the limit is weak unless the parent blocks it. [INFERENCE] I could not confirm that Screen Time can limit one Home Screen web app. I also could not confirm what it does to a web app that is open at the limit. [UNVERIFIED: test on the iPhone]
- **Fit.** No code, and the parent keeps full control. But the stop cuts a song, which the AAP tip sheet advises against, and the end has no pals and no ritual. The stop does come from the technology, which Hiniker 2016 found better than a parent stop. Guided Access in a Home Screen web app comes from a third-party report, and nobody tested it on the iPhone 12 yet (`pile-pals/docs/research/native-feel/shell.md`, section 6).
- **Effort.** None in code. One parent setup step.
- **Best use.** A backstop. Set a Guided Access Time Limit that is longer than the concert, for example 30 minutes. Then it fires only when the in-game end fails. [INFERENCE]

### Option 5: a soft warning with no end

- **What the child sees.** A pal cue, for example "last song", but the game does not stop. The parent ends the session.
- **Fit.** This is the weakest option. Spoken warnings from parents went with more upset in Hiniker 2016. A warning that the game does not keep teaches her that warnings mean nothing. [INFERENCE] The warning helps only as a part of option 1.
- **Effort.** About 2 hours.

### Recommendation

Build option 1, the concert of N songs with a goodbye ritual. Let the parent choose N (default 3, about 10 minutes) and an optional bedtime (option 3). Use a Guided Access Time Limit (option 4) only as a backstop that is longer than the concert. Keep the current rules that avoid compulsion: no autoplay, no streaks, no random rewards, stars never lost. Add one rule: a pal never asks her to stay and never looks sad when the concert ends.
